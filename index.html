Case study · Time series · MLOps

NEM Forecast

Half-hourly electricity demand forecasts for Victoria (VIC1) in Australia's National Electricity Market, 30 minutes to 24 hours ahead, each with a 10th–90th percentile range.

Why it's harder than it looks

  • Rooftop solar removes a weather-dependent slice of demand in the middle of the day, so the hardest hours are now around noon, not the evening peak.
  • AEMO keeps its clock on fixed AEST while people live on daylight-saving time.
  • Published values are revised after the fact.
  • A forecast for tomorrow may only use what was known today, a rule that lag features make easy to break silently.

What I built

  • Ingestion of AEMO's public 5-minute data every half hour, and a walk-forward backtest over a full year: 840,120 forecasts per model, scored against four honest baselines (same time yesterday, same time last week, the mean of the last four same weekdays, and persistence).
  • LightGBM quantile models, experiments tracked in MLflow on DagsHub, and a model card published on Hugging Face, versioned by git SHA.
  • A FastAPI service in Docker that records every forecast it serves, released through a pipeline of 365 tests, strict type checks and security scans.
  • Live monitoring: served forecasts are scored against actual demand, with error within 3% of what the backtest predicts for the same horizons and times of day.
  • Evidently drift checks with thresholds calibrated on two years of weekly history. The default threshold would have fired every single week; calibration cut false alarms from 100% to 6% of weeks.
  • Automatic retraining: a new model reaches production only by beating the served one at every horizon.

What the numbers taught me

  • The midday solar trough is the hardest time of day for every model, at roughly 3–4× the overnight error, because nothing in the model can see cloud cover. Weather data is the obvious next feature.
  • 50 Optuna trials improved validation MAE by only 3.6%. The features matter far more than the hyperparameters.

Case study · Multi-agent · LLM

ResearchForge AI

An autonomous research platform: submit a question and a team of specialised agents plans the research, finds and reads papers, verifies claims, argues about the conclusions and writes a citation-grounded report.

The agents

  1. Planner breaks the question into subtasks and picks a search strategy.
  2. Researcher searches arXiv, Semantic Scholar and Crossref for relevant papers.
  3. Synthesizer extracts key findings into a grounded synthesis, and writes the final report.
  4. Fact-Checker verifies important claims against the retrieved evidence.
  5. Debate agents argue competing conclusions from several perspectives.
  6. Critic checks completeness and can send the run back for more research.
  7. Evaluator scores the output on relevance, accuracy, completeness and citation quality.

Engineering highlights

  • Cut runtime from 3–4 minutes to about 1 minute by reducing LLM calls from 15 to 7 and running independent agents in parallel.
  • JWT/bcrypt login, per-user data isolation, Redis rate limiting and live progress updates while a run is in flight.
  • Deployed at $0/month on Vercel, Render and Neon.
  • GitHub Actions runs linting, type checks, dependency scans and 266 tests, including Postgres integration tests.
  • Also in the repo: Docker multi-stage builds, structured JSON logging with OpenTelemetry tracing, security headers (CORS, CSP, HSTS), and Terraform modules for an AWS deployment.

Case study · GraphRAG · Retrieval

Knowledge Graph RAG

Vector search struggles with questions that chain facts across documents, like "Who supplies the companies that compete with NVIDIA?" This system extracts a knowledge graph from SEC 10-K filings and routes every question to graph templates, vector search, or both.

How it works

  • Ingestion: 10-K sections (Business, Risk Factors, MD&A) are chunked, and an LLM extracts relationships against a fixed ontology. Every fact must carry a quote that appears verbatim in its chunk, or it is rejected.
  • Two stores, one key: Neo4j edges list the chunk ids that justify them, and pgvector rows list the entities their chunk mentions. Adding the company and section header to each embedded chunk raised recall@5 by 16 points.
  • Routing: one light LLM call chooses graph or vector and fills a parameterized Cypher template. The model never writes Cypher, and a test pushes an injection string through every template.
  • Grounded answers: a draft is rejected and regenerated when a claim has no citation, cites a source that was not retrieved, or states a number its cited text does not contain.

Scaled-up build

  • 25 filings from 20 semiconductor companies: 1,934 chunks, 1,184 entities and 1,598 facts.
  • Time-aware facts (current, past or planned), so sold subsidiaries and former executives are marked as ended.
  • Louvain community detection groups companies into industry clusters for overview questions.
  • Can route and answer questions fully offline with a local qwen3:8b model through Ollama on an 8 GB laptop GPU.
  • A web interface for non-technical readers: plain-language answers, a diagram of the facts each answer connected, and every source with the supporting sentence highlighted.
